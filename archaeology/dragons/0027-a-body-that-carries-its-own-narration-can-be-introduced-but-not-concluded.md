# A body that carries its own narration can be introduced but not concluded

## Context

A protocol and a machine both carry their own narration, and both allow an entry-level `say:` to
become a **prologue** — spoken before anything happens, over the cast or over the map. The lowering
appends the body's own occurrences *after* it, which is what makes the prologue a prologue.

There is no matching epilogue, and a machine wants one more than a protocol does. The closing shot
of a `circuit` is the whole machine with the run's wake on it and the traveller wherever it
stopped, and it is precisely the frame a sentence like "and none of that is where it was going"
belongs over. Both examples get there by writing the closing remark into the **last occurrence's**
`say:`, which works and is not the same thing: the sentence is then spoken over the last traversal
and the pull-back happens under its tail, rather than the sentence being spoken over the pull-back.

## Question

Should a body that carries its own narration be able to say something after it, and if so, what
distinguishes an epilogue from "a long sentence on the last occurrence"?

## Constraints

The obvious spelling — a second key beside `say:` — is two narration lists on one entry, and every
part of the format that has more than one of something has needed a rule about ordering. The
alternative reading is that `say:` on such an entry is simply *narration in slide order*, with the
body's occurrences spliced in where the author put a marker, which is a much larger change and
looks like reinventing `enter:`.

There is also a real argument for leaving it alone. decision:22's atlas asks for its reveal by
writing a closing cue that reaches no entity, and the author gets the pull-back by writing a
sentence and enough `post_say` to hold it. A machine has no such cue available, because every cue
it has is an occurrence. Whether that is a gap or a difference is the question.

## Resolution criteria

Close as **needed** if a third machine or protocol is authored whose ending is visibly worse for
having to be written into its last occurrence. Close as **not needed** if the "write it into the
last occurrence" idiom reads well across several more artifacts, in which case document it as the
idiom rather than leaving it to be rediscovered.
